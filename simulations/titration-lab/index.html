Titration Lab: help

How to run a titration

The flask holds the sample (the analyte). The buret holds a solution of known concentration (the titrant). Add titrant with the buttons under the bench, or hold Hold to pour. Go quickly at first, then drop by drop as the color starts to linger.

The graph, the particle views and the math all update with every drop.

Reading the particle views

  • Each small white bump on a particle is an acidic hydrogen (a proton it can give away). Watch the bumps move from acid to base.
  • Each particle stands for the same amount of substance. The legend gives the count of each kind. Anything too scarce to earn one whole particle is listed as "trace".
  • Water is left out unless you tick Show water molecules. In the real flask there are hundreds of water molecules for every ion.

Reading the buret

A buret is numbered from the top down: it tells you how much has been delivered. Read the bottom of the meniscus and estimate one digit past the smallest marking, to the nearest 0.01 mL. Volume added = final reading − starting reading.

Glossary

Equivalence point
The exact volume at which moles of titrant added equal the moles needed to react with all of the sample. It is a fact about amounts, not about pH.
Endpoint
Where the indicator changes color. A good indicator makes the endpoint land on the equivalence point.
Half-equivalence point
Half the equivalence volume. Half of a weak acid has been converted to its conjugate base, so [HA] = [A⁻] and pH = pKa.
Buffer region
The flat stretch before equivalence in a weak acid or weak base titration. Both members of a conjugate pair are present, so added acid or base barely moves the pH.
Strong vs. weak
Strong acids and bases ionize completely in water. Weak ones ionize only slightly, so most of the molecules stay intact.
Spectator ion
An ion that is present but takes no part in the reaction (Na⁺ and Cl⁻ here).

How real is it? (for teachers)

  • The pH is found by solving the full charge-balance equation for every mixture (Kw = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴, 25 °C), so there are no approximations in the curve. "The math" card shows the standard classroom method beside it.
  • One drop is 0.05 mL, the buret holds 50.00 mL with 0.1 mL markings, and the flask level rises by the true volume for a 250 mL Erlenmeyer flask.
  • Indicator colors change over their real pH ranges. Near the endpoint the color flashes where the drop lands and fades on stirring, as it does in the lab.
  • Simplified: activity effects are ignored, the pH meter responds instantly and has no drift, and carbonic acid does not escape as CO₂.
